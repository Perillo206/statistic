Part 1 — Encryption and decryption with the toy secp256k1 scheme

The scheme is an EC-ElGamal / ECIES-style hybrid on secp256k1 (y² = x³ + 7 over Fp, p = 2²⁵⁶ − 2³² − 977, generator G, group order n). Because it is a toy, the private key is restricted to a small range [1, 2b − 1].

  1. Key generation. Pick a private key d in the small range. The public key is Q = d·G.
  2. Encryption. Pick a fresh random scalar r. Compute C₁ = r·G (sent with the message) and the shared point S = r·Q. The keystream is the 64 bytes of S.x ‖ S.y; the ciphertext is C₂ = message ⊕ keystream.
  3. Decryption. The receiver computes S = d·C₁ = d·r·G = r·Q, rebuilds the same keystream and XORs again.

Role of the keys. Q is public and lets anyone encrypt. d is secret and is the only trapdoor to recompute S from C₁. Security rests on the difficulty of finding d from Q (ECDLP) or S from C₁ and Q (CDH); r is a one-time secret that makes each ciphertext different.

Part 2 — Caesar cipher (same message)

Each letter is moved s positions forward in the alphabet: C = (P + s) mod 26, and decryption uses P = (C − s) mod 26. Case and punctuation are kept; accents are removed (è → e). We use the 26-letter alphabet; letters absent from Italian (J, K, W, X, Y) simply have frequency ≈ 0.

4.2 Procedure (as designed)

Method A — top-5 letter matching. Count the letters of the ciphertext and keep the 5 most frequent. Compare them with the 5 most frequent Italian letters (E, A, I, O, N). Hypothesise that the most frequent ciphertext letter is E and compute the shift s = (cipher − E) mod 26; decrypt and check whether the text becomes Italian. If not, try the next pairing (cipher #1 ↔ A, cipher #2 ↔ E, …) until the decryption is readable. “Readable” is tested automatically: at least 35% of the words are common Italian words and at least 3 of the decrypted top-5 letters are in {E, A, I, O, N}.

Method B — single-letter words. While scanning, note every word made of one letter. In Italian the only one-letter words are a, e, i, o. If four different single-letter ciphertext words are found, they must correspond to these four letters, and only one shift maps the set of four onto {A, E, I, O}. That shift is computed directly, without trying any decryption. With fewer than four distinct words, the same test narrows the list of possible shifts.

Brute force. Decrypt with all 26 shifts and pick the one that scores best (here: dictionary hits). It needs no statistics on the cipher letters but always costs 26 decryptions and 26 scorings.

Part 5 — Attacking the toy ECC scheme (restricted private key)

The private key lies in [1, 2b). An attacker who sees Q and C₁ can recover d without any statistics, because the whole key space can be enumerated.

Attack 1 — exhaustive search. Start at G and keep adding G: kG for k = 1, 2, … until kG = Q. One point addition per candidate, at most 2b additions. Attack 2 — baby-step giant-step (BSGS). With m = ⌈√2b⌉, store jG for j = 1…m, then walk Q − i·mG until it hits the table: d = i·m + j. About 2·√2b steps.

Differences from the Caesar attack

  • The Caesar attack is statistical: it exploits the fact that the ciphertext keeps the language's letter distribution. The ECC attack is purely algebraic/exhaustive: the ciphertext leaks nothing statistical, only the tiny key range is exploited, and success is exact (the check kG = Q is deterministic).
  • Caesar needs a long enough text and may fail on short ones; the ECC attack works on any message length, including one byte.
  • Against a proper scheme (d ~ 2²⁵⁶) exhaustive search needs about 2²⁵⁶ additions and BSGS about 2¹²⁸ steps and memory, so neither is feasible. The best known generic attack (Pollard's ρ) still costs about 2¹²⁸, which is why 256-bit curves give roughly 128-bit security.

Optional: other weaknesses

  • From the restricted key space: exhaustive search, BSGS or Pollard's ρ recover d; precomputed tables of dG can be reused for every victim.
  • From the design of the toy scheme itself: the raw x‖y of S is used as keystream (no KDF), so a message longer than 64 bytes reuses the keystream (two-time pad); the ciphertext is malleable (flip a bit in C₂ and the same bit flips in the plaintext) because there is no MAC; known plaintext exposes the keystream directly; reusing r would reveal the XOR of two messages; and invalid-curve or small-subgroup issues are avoided only because secp256k1 has cofactor 1.
